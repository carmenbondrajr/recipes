# 🧀 Cacio e Pepe

*One skillet + one small pot, ~15 minutes, serves 1. Just three ingredients: pasta, Pecorino Romano, and black pepper. The trick is getting them to turn into a creamy sauce instead of a stringy clump.*

## Shopping List

**Dairy**
- **Pecorino Romano, a block** (smallest wedge, ~4–6 oz, you'll use 2 oz) — **a block, not pre-grated.** *Pre-grated cheese is coated in anti-caking powder (cellulose) and won't melt smoothly. It's the #1 reason this dish fails. Look for "Pecorino Romano" specifically, the sheep's milk cheese, not Parmesan*

**Pantry / Center Aisles**
- Spaghetti, **regular thickness** (1 lb box, you'll use 4 oz), or **tonnarelli / bucatini** if you see them — *tonnarelli is the traditional Roman shape, a square-edged thick spaghetti*
- **Whole black peppercorns** + a grinder that can go coarse — *pre-ground pepper is dusty and flat. It's half the dish's name, so this matters*

*You should have at home: salt, butter (optional)*

**Equipment check:** a microplane or the fine side of a box grater. Finely grated cheese melts fast and evenly, while coarse shreds go stringy.

---

## Recipe

**Ingredients**
- 4 oz (115 g) spaghetti
- 2 oz (55 g) Pecorino Romano, **finely grated** (~1 cup fluffy, using a microplane)
- 1½ tsp coarsely cracked black pepper
- Salt for the pasta water, **less than usual**

**Steps**

1. **Grate the cheese first** (~3 min). Finely grate the **2 oz Pecorino** into a bowl. Grate a little extra for the table if you want. Have everything ready, because once the pasta is done this moves fast.

2. **Crack and toast the pepper** (~2 min). Crack the **1½ tsp pepper** coarse, like gravel and not dust. Toast it in a **dry 10-inch skillet** over medium for about 1 minute, until it smells fragrant and spicy. Heat wakes up the pepper's aromatic oils. Pull the skillet off the burner.

3. **Cook the pasta in less water than usual** (~9 min). Bring **about 1 quart of water** to a boil in a small pot with only **½ tsp salt**. **Pecorino is very salty**, and normal pasta-water salt will push the dish over the edge. Add the **4 oz spaghetti** and stir often for the first minute so it doesn't stick. **Less water means starchier water**, and that starch is the glue that holds the sauce together. Cook **2 minutes short of the box time**, because it finishes in the skillet.

4. **Make the cheese paste** (~1 min, while the pasta cooks). About 5 minutes into the pasta, scoop **¼ cup of the starchy water** out and let it cool for a minute. Add it to the **grated Pecorino** a little at a time, mashing with a fork, until it's a thick, smooth paste like loose frosting. **Why a paste:** cheese hydrated *before* it meets the hot pasta melts smoothly instead of seizing into clumps.

5. **Finish the pasta in the pepper skillet** (~2 min). Before you drain, **scoop out 1 more cup of pasta water** and keep it handy. Put the skillet with the pepper back on medium, add **½ cup of pasta water**, and transfer the **spaghetti** in with tongs. Toss for 1–2 minutes until the pasta is al dente and the water has reduced to a couple of tablespoons of cloudy, starchy liquid.

6. **Off the heat, then add the cheese** (~1 min). **Take the skillet off the burner and wait 30 seconds.** This is the most important step in the recipe. Pecorino seizes into rubbery strings if it hits a pan that's too hot. Scrape in the **cheese paste** and toss hard and fast with tongs, swirling the pan, until the sauce turns creamy and coats every strand. Splash in **pasta water a tablespoon at a time** if it's thick or pasty. It should look glossy and pool slightly at the bottom of the pan.

7. **Eat immediately.** Twirl it into a warm bowl, crack more pepper on top, and add extra Pecorino if you grated it. The sauce tightens within a couple of minutes, so don't let it sit.

## Tips & Notes
- **What ruins this: clumped, stringy cheese.** Three possible causes, in order: (1) **pan too hot** when the cheese went in, (2) **pre-grated cheese**, (3) **not enough starch** in the water. What "wrong" looks like: white rubbery strands glued to the tongs and a watery puddle underneath. Prevention beats rescue, but if it starts happening, splash in more pasta water and keep tossing off the heat. Sometimes it comes back.
- **Too salty?** That's usually the pasta water. Next time use less salt. Romans salt the water barely at all for this dish.
- **Butter insurance:** a **tablespoon of butter** melted into the pepper water in step 5 makes the sauce more forgiving and creamier. Roman purists would be horrified. It's genuinely easier, though, and great for your first try.
- **Too peppery?** Drop to 1 tsp. Coarse cracking is less fiery than fine grinding, so if you're using a fine grinder, use less.
- **Pecorino vs. Parmesan:** you can swap in Parmesan or do half and half. It's milder, nuttier, and a little easier to melt. Then it's not quite cacio e pepe anymore. Parmesan plus butter is basically the original Roman fettuccine Alfredo.
- **Scaling up:** for 2, double everything but keep the skillet hot enough to reduce the water. Cacio e pepe gets harder at bigger batches, which makes it a perfect dinner for one.
- **Name time:** *cacio* is Roman (and southern Italian) dialect for cheese, from the Latin *caseus*, which is also where "cheese" and *queso* come from. So it's literally "cheese and pepper."
- **Shepherd food.** The story goes that this is what Roman-countryside shepherds packed when they drove flocks to seasonal pastures. Hard aged pecorino, dried pasta, and peppercorns all keep for weeks without refrigeration and weigh almost nothing. Its siblings *gricia* (add guanciale) and *carbonara* (add egg) are the same idea, built out.
