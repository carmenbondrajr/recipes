# 🐷 Tonkatsu

*One skillet, ~40 minutes including the rice, serves 1. A panko-crusted pork cutlet, fried shatter-crisp and served over a pile of icy shredded cabbage with tangy-sweet tonkatsu sauce and rice.*

## Shopping List

**Produce**
- Cabbage: a **small wedge of green or napa cabbage**, or a **bag of plain shredded cabbage / coleslaw mix** — *store-time call: the bag saves 5 minutes of knife work, but whole cabbage shaved super thin is crunchier and is the traditional way*
- Lemon (1) — *optional, a wedge for squeezing*
- Scallion (1) — *optional garnish*

**Meat**
- **One boneless pork loin chop, ¾ to 1 inch thick** (~6–8 oz). *Packs labeled "center-cut boneless loin chop" are perfect. Avoid anything under ½ inch, because it overcooks before the crust gets golden. Pork tenderloin also works (see Tips).*

**Dairy / Eggs**
- Eggs (you need 1)

**Pantry / Center Aisles**
- **Panko** (one 8 oz box, you'll use ~1 cup) — *Japanese-style, not regular breadcrumbs. Panko's big airy flakes are what make the crust shatter instead of crunch*
- **Tonkatsu sauce**, **Bull-Dog** brand is the classic (Asian aisle), **or** skip it and make it from pantry stuff (see the sauce in Steps)
- Short-grain or medium-grain rice like **Calrose** (any bag) — *long-grain like jasmine works too, it's just less sticky*
- Neutral oil, vegetable or canola (you need ~1½ cups) — *check how much you have at home*
- Toasted sesame seeds — *optional, for grinding into the sauce like tonkatsu restaurants do*

*You should have at home: all-purpose flour, salt, pepper. For homemade sauce: ketchup, Worcestershire, soy sauce, sugar*

**Equipment check:** an instant-read thermometer helps a lot here, both for the oil and for the pork. A wire rack set over a plate or sheet pan for draining.

## Recipe

**Ingredients**
- 1 boneless pork loin chop (¾–1 inch, ~6–8 oz)
- ½ tsp kosher salt + black pepper
- ¼ cup all-purpose flour
- 1 egg
- 1 cup panko
- ~1½ cups neutral oil, enough for ½ inch in a 10-inch skillet
- 2 cups finely shredded cabbage
- ¾ cup dry rice (makes ~2 cups cooked, a little extra is fine)

**Tonkatsu sauce, if making your own**
- 2 tbsp ketchup
- 1 tbsp Worcestershire
- 1 tsp soy sauce
- 1 tsp sugar

**Steps**

1. **Start the rice** (~25 min, hands-off). Rinse the **¾ cup rice** in a strainer until the water runs mostly clear. That washes off loose surface starch so the grains don't turn gummy. Cook it however you normally do (rice cooker or stovetop) and forget about it.

2. **Shred and ice the cabbage** (~5 min). Slice the cabbage **as thin as you can**, paper-thin shreds and not chunky slaw. Drop the **2 cups** into a bowl of ice water and let it sit until you plate. The cold water makes it crisp and curly and takes the raw sulfur edge off. Bagged cabbage benefits from the ice bath too.

3. **Make the sauce** (~1 min). Stir together the **2 tbsp ketchup, 1 tbsp Worcestershire, 1 tsp soy sauce, and 1 tsp sugar** (or pour out some Bull-Dog). *Optional restaurant move:* crush **1 tbsp toasted sesame seeds** in a mortar or with the bottom of a mug and stir them in. It makes the sauce nutty and wonderful.

4. **Prep the chop** (~3 min). Pat the **chop** dry. Make 3–4 small cuts through the **fat and connective tissue along the edge**, about ½ inch apart. That band shrinks faster than the meat when it heats, and unscored it pulls the chop into a curled-up bowl that won't brown evenly. If the chop is thicker than 1 inch, put it between plastic wrap and pound it with a pan to an even ¾ inch. Season both sides with **½ tsp salt** and pepper.

5. **Bread it: flour → egg → panko** (~3 min). Set up three shallow bowls: **¼ cup flour**, **1 egg** beaten with a splash of water, and **1 cup panko**. Coat the chop in flour and shake off the excess. The flour gives the egg something dry to grip. Dip it in egg and let the drips fall off. Lay it in the panko and **press firmly** on both sides so the crumbs really stick. Set it on a plate and **let it rest 5 minutes** while the oil heats. The coating hydrates and sets, so it won't slide off in the oil.

6. **Heat the oil to 340°F** (~5 min). Pour about **½ inch of oil** (~1½ cups) into a heavy 10-inch skillet over medium. **No thermometer?** Drop in one panko crumb. It should sizzle right away and float, but not brown in a few seconds.

7. **Fry** (~6–8 min). Lay the chop in the oil *away* from you. Fry **3–4 minutes per side**, flipping once, until it's deep golden all over. Spoon some hot oil over the top while the first side cooks so it starts to set. Keep the oil around 325–350°F by adjusting the burner, because the chop going in will drop the temperature. **Pull it at 140°F** in the center. It'll carry over to 145°F as it rests.

8. **Drain on a rack, not paper towels** (~3 min). Stand the chop on its edge on the wire rack for a minute, then lay it flat. Paper towels trap steam under the cutlet and the bottom crust goes soggy. A rack lets air all the way around. Rest **3 minutes**.

9. **Slice and plate.** Cut it crosswise into ¾-inch strips with a sharp knife, using **one firm downward cut** instead of sawing, which rips the crust off. Drain the cabbage well and pile it high next to the katsu and rice. Drizzle sauce over the cutlet (or dip as you go), squeeze a little lemon on the cabbage, and eat.

## Tips & Notes
- **What ruins this: greasy, soggy breading.** That almost always means the **oil was too cold**. The panko soaks up oil instead of frying in it. If the chop goes in and the bubbling is lazy, turn the heat up and wait. Second most common cause: draining on paper towels.
- **Breading falling off?** Either the chop was wet before the flour, or the panko wasn't pressed in and rested. Pat dry, press hard, wait the 5 minutes.
- **Burnt outside, raw inside?** Oil too hot (over 375°F), or the chop is thicker than 1 inch. Pound it thinner next time.
- **Don't be scared of the oil.** Half an inch in a skillet is shallow-frying, not deep-frying. Use a skillet with tall-ish sides and keep a lid nearby. Let the used oil cool, strain it into a jar, and you can fry with it once or twice more.
- **Tenderloin version (hire-katsu):** cut a pork tenderloin into 1-inch medallions, pound each to ¾ inch, and fry 2–3 min per side. It's leaner and more tender, and it's what *hire-katsu* means on Japanese menus.
- **Leftovers become katsudon tomorrow.** Simmer sliced leftover katsu with onion in a little soy-sweetened broth, pour a beaten egg over it, cover until just set, and slide it all onto rice. It's arguably better than the original.
- **The cabbage isn't just garnish.** It's there to cut through the fried richness. At tonkatsu restaurants in Japan it's usually unlimited and refilled for free, often with a sesame dressing.
- **Name time:** *ton* (豚) means pork, and *katsu* is short for *katsuretsu*, the Japanese pronunciation of "cutlet." It started as *yōshoku*, Western-style food adapted in Meiji-era Japan. Tokyo's Rengatei restaurant is credited with an early pork cutlet in 1899. Because *katsu* also sounds like "to win" (勝つ), students eat it for luck before big exams.
- **Panko is Japanese for "bread crumbs."** *Pan* (bread, borrowed from the Portuguese *pão* in the 1500s) + *ko* (flour/powder).
