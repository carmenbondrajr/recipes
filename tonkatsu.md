# 🐷 Tonkatsu

*One skillet, ~50 minutes including the rice, serves 4. Panko-crusted pork cutlets, fried shatter-crisp and served over a pile of icy shredded cabbage with tangy-sweet tonkatsu sauce and rice.*

## Shopping List

**Produce**
- Cabbage: **half a small head of green or napa cabbage**, or **one 14–16 oz bag of plain shredded cabbage / coleslaw mix** — *store-time call: the bag saves 10 minutes of knife work, but whole cabbage shaved super thin is crunchier and is the traditional way*
- Lemon (1) — *optional, wedges for squeezing*
- Scallions (2) — *optional garnish*

**Meat**
- **Four boneless pork loin chops, ¾ to 1 inch thick** (~1½–2 lb total, usually one pack). *Packs labeled "center-cut boneless loin chops" are perfect. Avoid anything under ½ inch, because it overcooks before the crust gets golden. Pork tenderloin also works (see Tips).*

**Dairy / Eggs**
- Eggs (you need 2)

**Pantry / Center Aisles**
- **Panko** (one 8 oz box, you'll use ~3 cups, most of it) — *Japanese-style, not regular breadcrumbs. Panko's big airy flakes are what make the crust shatter instead of crunch*
- **Tonkatsu sauce**, **Bull-Dog** brand is the classic (Asian aisle), **or** skip it and make it from pantry stuff (see the sauce in Steps)
- Short-grain or medium-grain rice like **Calrose** (you need 2 cups dry) — *long-grain like jasmine works too, it's just less sticky*
- Neutral oil, vegetable or canola (you need ~2 cups) — *check how much you have at home*
- Toasted sesame seeds — *optional, for grinding into the sauce like tonkatsu restaurants do*

*You should have at home: all-purpose flour, kosher salt, pepper. For homemade sauce: ketchup, Worcestershire, soy sauce, sugar*

**Equipment check:** an instant-read thermometer helps a lot here, both for the oil and for the pork. A wire rack set in a sheet pan for draining and keeping the first batch warm.

## Recipe

**Ingredients**
- 4 boneless pork loin chops (¾–1 inch, ~1½–2 lb)
- 2 tsp kosher salt + black pepper
- ½ cup all-purpose flour
- 2 eggs
- 3 cups panko
- ~2 cups neutral oil, enough for ½ inch in a 12-inch skillet
- ~8 cups finely shredded cabbage
- 2 cups dry rice (makes ~6 cups cooked)

**Tonkatsu sauce, if making your own**
- ½ cup ketchup
- ¼ cup Worcestershire
- 4 tsp soy sauce
- 4 tsp sugar

**Steps**

1. **Start the rice** (~25 min, hands-off). Rinse the **2 cups rice** in a strainer until the water runs mostly clear. That washes off loose surface starch so the grains don't turn gummy. Cook it however you normally do (rice cooker or stovetop) and forget about it.

2. **Shred and ice the cabbage** (~10 min). Slice the cabbage **as thin as you can**, paper-thin shreds and not chunky slaw. Drop the **~8 cups** into a big bowl of ice water and let it sit until you plate. The cold water makes it crisp and curly and takes the raw sulfur edge off. Bagged cabbage benefits from the ice bath too.

3. **Make the sauce** (~1 min). Stir together the **½ cup ketchup, ¼ cup Worcestershire, 4 tsp soy sauce, and 4 tsp sugar** (or pour out some Bull-Dog). *Optional restaurant move:* crush **2 tbsp toasted sesame seeds** in a mortar or with the bottom of a mug and stir them in. It makes the sauce nutty and wonderful.

4. **Prep the chops** (~5 min). Heat the oven to **200°F** with the rack-in-sheet-pan inside. That's where the first batch stays crisp while the second fries. Pat the **4 chops** dry. Make 3–4 small cuts through the **fat and connective tissue along each edge**, about ½ inch apart. That band shrinks faster than the meat when it heats, and unscored it pulls each chop into a curled-up bowl that won't brown evenly. If they're thicker than 1 inch, put them between plastic wrap and pound with a pan to an even ¾ inch. Season both sides with the **2 tsp salt** (about ½ tsp per chop) and pepper.

5. **Bread them: flour → egg → panko** (~5 min). Set up three shallow bowls: **½ cup flour**, **2 eggs** beaten with a splash of water, and **3 cups panko**. Coat each chop in flour and shake off the excess. The flour gives the egg something dry to grip. Dip in egg and let the drips fall off. Lay it in the panko and **press firmly** on both sides so the crumbs really stick. Set them on a plate and **let them rest 5 minutes** while the oil heats. The coating hydrates and sets, so it won't slide off in the oil.

6. **Heat the oil to 340°F** (~5 min). Pour about **½ inch of oil** (~2 cups) into a heavy 12-inch skillet over medium. **No thermometer?** Drop in one panko crumb. It should sizzle right away and float, but not brown in a few seconds.

7. **Fry in two batches** (~7 min per batch). Lay **2 chops** in the oil, *away* from you. Fry **3–4 minutes per side**, flipping once, until deep golden all over. Spoon some hot oil over the tops while the first side cooks so they start to set. **Pull at 140°F** in the center. They'll carry over to 145°F as they rest. Move them to the rack in the warm oven. Let the oil climb back to 340°F before the next 2 go in. **Skipping that reheat is how batch two goes greasy.**

8. **Drain on a rack, not paper towels.** The rack in the oven does double duty. Paper towels trap steam under the cutlets and the bottom crust goes soggy. A rack lets air all the way around. Rest the last batch **3 minutes**.

9. **Slice and plate.** Cut each cutlet crosswise into ¾-inch strips with a sharp knife, using **one firm downward cut** instead of sawing, which rips the crust off. Drain the cabbage well and pile it high next to the katsu and rice. Drizzle sauce over the cutlets (or dip as you go), squeeze a little lemon on the cabbage, and eat.

## Tips & Notes
- **What ruins this: greasy, soggy breading.** That almost always means the **oil was too cold**. The panko soaks up oil instead of frying in it. If the chops go in and the bubbling is lazy, turn the heat up and wait. Second most common cause: draining on paper towels.
- **Breading falling off?** Either the chops were wet before the flour, or the panko wasn't pressed in and rested. Pat dry, press hard, wait the 5 minutes.
- **Burnt outside, raw inside?** Oil too hot (over 375°F), or the chops are thicker than 1 inch. Pound them thinner next time.
- **Don't be scared of the oil.** Half an inch in a skillet is shallow-frying, not deep-frying. Use a skillet with tall-ish sides and keep a lid nearby. Let the used oil cool, strain it into a jar, and you can fry with it once or twice more.
- **Leftovers: store the katsu, cabbage, and sauce separately.** Reheat cutlets on a rack at **400°F for ~10 minutes** (or 5–6 min in an air fryer) to bring the crunch back. **Never microwave**, because the breading turns to wet cardboard. Keep the cabbage dry in a container with a paper towel and it stays crisp for 2–3 days.
- **Or turn leftovers into katsudon.** Simmer sliced leftover katsu with onion in a little soy-sweetened broth, pour a beaten egg over it, cover until just set, and slide it all onto rice. It's arguably better than the original.
- **Tenderloin version (hire-katsu):** cut a pork tenderloin into 1-inch medallions, pound each to ¾ inch, and fry 2–3 min per side. It's leaner and more tender, and it's what *hire-katsu* means on Japanese menus.
- **The cabbage isn't just garnish.** It's there to cut through the fried richness. At tonkatsu restaurants in Japan it's usually unlimited and refilled for free, often with a sesame dressing.
- **Name time:** *ton* (豚) means pork, and *katsu* is short for *katsuretsu*, the Japanese pronunciation of "cutlet." It started as *yōshoku*, Western-style food adapted in Meiji-era Japan. Tokyo's Rengatei restaurant is credited with an early pork cutlet in 1899. Because *katsu* also sounds like "to win" (勝つ), students eat it for luck before big exams.
- **Panko is Japanese for "bread crumbs."** *Pan* (bread, borrowed from the Portuguese *pão* in the 1500s) + *ko* (flour/powder).
