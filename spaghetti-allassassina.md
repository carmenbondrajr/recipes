# 🔥 Spaghetti all'Assassina

*One skillet, ~30 minutes, serves 2 (or 1 very hungry person). Dry spaghetti cooked like risotto in spicy tomato broth, scorched on purpose until it's crispy, charred, and chewy.*

## Shopping List

**Produce**
- Garlic (1 head, or use the jar)

**Pantry / Center Aisles**
- Spaghetti, **regular thickness** (1 lb box, you'll use ~7 oz / a bit under half) — *not thin spaghetti or angel hair, which burn through before they cook*
- Tomato passata (one 24 oz bottle / ~3 cups, you'll use 2 cups) — *passata is smooth strained tomato. If the store doesn't have it, use a 28 oz can of **tomato purée**, not crushed or diced. Chunks burn before the pasta cooks*
- Tomato paste (tube) — *you only need 1 tbsp*
- Dried whole chiles (small bag, like chiles de árbol), **or** just use red pepper flakes from home — *whole chiles are more traditional and fry nicely in the oil*

*You should have at home: olive oil, salt, sugar, red pepper flakes*

**Equipment check:** a **12-inch cast iron, carbon steel, or stainless skillet**. Nonstick works, but you get less char, and the char is the whole dish.

---

## Recipe

**Ingredients**
- 7 oz (200 g) spaghetti, regular thickness
- 2 cups passata, split: 3 tbsp for the pan, the rest for the broth
- 3 cups water
- 1 tbsp tomato paste
- 1 tsp salt + a pinch of sugar
- ¼ cup olive oil
- 3 garlic cloves, smashed (or 1½ tsp jarred)
- 2–3 dried chiles, broken up, **or** 1 tsp red pepper flakes (½ tsp if you're cautious)

**Steps**

1. **Make the tomato broth** (~5 min). In a small saucepan, whisk the **1¾ cups passata** (everything except 3 tbsp), **3 cups water**, **1 tbsp tomato paste**, **1 tsp salt**, and a **pinch of sugar**. Bring it to a simmer and keep it hot on a back burner the whole time. **Hot broth matters:** cold broth drops the skillet temperature every time you ladle it in, and the pasta steams instead of frying.

2. **Bloom the chile and garlic** (~2 min). Heat the **¼ cup olive oil** in the skillet over medium. Add the **3 garlic cloves** and **chiles/flakes** and let them sizzle gently until the garlic is just golden. Don't let it go brown, because burnt garlic tastes bitter and this dish already brings plenty of bitterness on purpose. Add the **3 reserved tbsp passata** and let it fry in the oil for a minute until it darkens and thickens. This concentrated tomato layer is what catches and chars first.

3. **Fry the dry pasta** (~2 min). Turn the heat to medium-high. Lay the **7 oz dry spaghetti** in the pan in as flat a single layer as you can. Break it in half if you need to (purists will forgive you). Let it sizzle in the oil for a minute, then turn it with tongs so the other side gets coated. You're toasting the starch before any liquid touches it. That's why it stays chewy instead of going soft.

4. **Ladle, wait, scorch, flip. Repeat.** (~15–18 min). Add **one ladle (~½ cup) of hot broth**, just enough to come about halfway up the pasta. Now **leave it alone.** It bubbles, the liquid disappears, and then the pan goes quiet and starts to crackle. That crackle is the bottom layer catching. Give it another 30–60 seconds, then flip the pasta in sections with tongs or a spatula and scrape up the stuck bits. Add another ladle. Keep going.
   - **What "right" looks like:** dark mahogany spots and crispy edges, and it smells toasty, like the burnt corner of a lasagna.
   - **What "wrong" looks like:** acrid gray smoke and black dust. Drop the heat a notch and ladle a little sooner next round.
   - Every instinct will tell you to stir. Don't. Stirring turns it into plain one-pot pasta.

5. **Check for doneness** (around minute 15). Bite a strand. It should be **al dente with crunchy bits**, chewier than normal pasta. If it's still hard in the middle, keep going with smaller ladles. Taste for salt and spice now. You may not use all the broth, and that's fine.

6. **Final crisp, then serve straight from the pan.** Let the last ladle cook off completely and leave it on the heat for one more minute so the bottom gets a good crust. Then flip it out onto plates crispy side up. It doesn't wait well. The crunch softens within about 10 minutes.

## Tips & Notes
- **What ruins this: low heat.** If the pan isn't hot enough, the broth just simmers and the pasta goes soft and gluey, like a sad one-pot spaghetti. You want an aggressive bubble and a crackle between ladles. If ten minutes in nothing has stuck to the pan, turn it up.
- **The second thing that ruins it: rushing the broth.** Two ladles at once means the pasta boils instead of frying. One at a time, and let each one fully disappear.
- **Burnt vs. charred.** Dark brown, sticky, and caramelized is the goal. Matte black and ashy is too far. A few black spots are traditional, though, so don't panic.
- **Spice level is yours.** In Bari it's *ferociously* hot. 1 tsp of flakes for two people is a solid kick. Start at ½ tsp if you're unsure, because you can't take it back out.
- **Solo version:** halve everything and use a 10-inch skillet. It's actually easier to get good char with less pasta crowding the pan.
- **Burrata on top?** It's the popular modern move, and cool creamy cheese against scorched spicy pasta is great. The purists (see below) say absolutely no cheese. Your kitchen, your rules.
- **Cleanup:** the pan will look like a crime scene. Fill it with hot water while you eat and it'll scrape right off.
- **The name means "the assassin's spaghetti."** The story goes that around 1967, at Al Sorso Preferito in Bari, chef Enzo Francavilla served it to two diners from northern Italy, and they called him an assassin over the heat. Bari takes it very seriously. There's an *Accademia dell'Assassina* with official rules, and one of them is that you never stir.
